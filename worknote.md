WORKSHOP NOTE · WHO CONTROLS, WHO ANSWERS?
The Unified Ex Ante Objection
The strongest form of the objection that a single unified regime would do this work sooner and more coherently, the second-order evasions a deployer turns to once the record duty binds, and the answers the manuscript already has the materials to give.

Zhang & Zhao, Harv. J.L. & Tech. (submission draft)

01 · The framing
The objection assumes an axis the Article does not occupy.
Centralization and decentralization describe the level at which a rule is enacted. The Article's two questions are when information is created — ex ante creation against ex post reconstruction — and for whom it is created — regulator-facing against person-facing. Choosing a level answers neither. A national or supranational legislature can adopt this architecture without changing a word of its substance, and a sub-national one can enact a regime that is purely regulator-facing.

The manuscript already says this three times — pp. 5, 35, 57 — but says it in subordinate clauses, at moments when the reader is thinking about Erie and preemption rather than about institutional design. A load-bearing claim is currently carried by asides.

Nothing in the answer requires treating any legal family as the baseline. The front door's closest institutional relatives sit in civil-law pre-action evidence procedure, and the Article's own administrability comparators are national. Both facts cut toward the same conclusion and both are currently unstated.

The three administrability comparators in Part IV.D — FAA Part 5, the FTC's 2025 § 6(b) companion-chatbot orders, and the FCRA — are all national regimes. An architecture offered as a workaround for the absence of central authority would not prove its own feasibility out of central authority's own instruments.
02 · Objection and answer
Four claims, stated at full strength.
Speed
One enactment covers every sector; a model act must be adopted repeatedly.
Objection
A single legislature can impose a record duty across sectors in one instrument. A model act needs repeated adoption, and every adoption reopens the field list at its most lobbyable point — the moment where the duty becomes specific and costly.

Answer
The rule's speed and the record's speed are different quantities.

The attribution gap is not produced by the absence of a rule. It is produced by records that were never created Part I.E; Part IV opening. A rule enacted quickly creates no record retroactively, any more than a rule enacted slowly does. The binding constraint sits at deployment time, and no level of enactment moves it.

The premise is also weaker than it looks on its own terms, and the enacting legislature has said why. The most complete unified ex ante architecture now in force deferred its core high-risk obligations well past their original date — standalone Annex III obligations from 2 August 2026 to 2 December 2027, Annex I obligations to 2 August 2028 — and recital 2 of the amending Regulation states the reason: "the delayed preparation of standards, which provide technical solutions for providers of high-risk AI systems to ensure compliance with their obligations under that Regulation, and the delayed establishment of the governance and the conformity assessment frameworks at national level has resulted in a compliance burden that is heavier than expected," with stakeholder consultations revealing "the need for additional measures to facilitate and provide clarification on implementation and compliance" Reg. (EU) 2026/1744, recital 2.

Unified enactment concentrates the decision; it cannot conjure the machinery that makes the decision operable. What slipped was not legislative will but implementation infrastructure — standards that did not exist and conformity-assessment bodies that had not been established. The shape of that failure is the Article's own thesis one level up: the binding constraint was not the absence of a rule but the absence of what the rule presupposed.

Read precisely, the recital does more than blunt the speed claim; it distinguishes two kinds of ex ante duty. What proved unaffordable on schedule was an approval architecture — obligations discharged through harmonised standards and national conformity assessment, inert until both exist. The front door depends on neither. The designation fixes a closed field list before the event, so what compliance requires is knowable on the day the statute passes; there is no notified body, no conformity assessment, no CE marking, no registration, and no standards body on whose timetable the duty waits. That is why Part IV.D's comparators are record regimes rather than conformity regimes. A description can be specified by a legislature in one sitting; a measure has to wait for someone to build the instrument.

Beneficiary
Unified regimes already mandate documentation, logging, and traceability.
Objection
Comprehensive ex ante regulation already requires technical documentation, automatic logging, and record retention across high-risk deployments. A private access route duplicates work the regulator has already commissioned.

Answer
The duties run in different directions, and neither substitutes for the other.

A regulator-facing duty resolves the state's opacity problem: the file exists and the agency can reach it. The affected person still faces exactly the problem the Article identifies — the record exists but is not hers, and reaching it depends on an intermediary's discretion and on procedures that presuppose an identified target.

The comparison is available inside a single instrument. The AI Act's Article 86 gives an affected person a right to clear and meaningful explanations of the role of the AI system in the decision-making procedure and the main elements of the decision taken, excluding Annex III point 2. That is an explanation right. It is not an event receipt, a version identification, a custodian map, or a control allocation. The litigation-facing counterpart — the proposed AI Liability Directive's court-ordered disclosure of evidence concerning high-risk systems — was withdrawn by the Commission and never enacted.

The manuscript already knows the distinction and buries it: p. 51 flags that FCRA § 1681m(h)(8) excludes private civil actions for § 1681m failures. The Article is choosing a more person-facing route than its own closest domestic model — visible at present only to a reader who follows the footnote.

Reach
Divided deployments are transnational; a sub-national rule cannot follow them.
Objection
Part V.C concedes the enacting State cannot reach an upstream supplier directly and must route through § 2's contractual retrieval right. A national or supranational regulator has no such problem. § 11's preemption and severability apparatus is itself evidence of how much design effort a sub-national rule spends simply to survive.

Answer
This is the objection's best point. Concede the cost, then bound it.

The contractual retrieval right is not a second-best substitute for jurisdiction. It is a different mechanism doing work jurisdiction does not do. Rule 34 control turns on a legal right to obtain documents on demand; § 2 creates that right before deployment rather than litigating it after harm pp. 24, 35. A regulator with complete territorial reach still cannot supply a right the operator never contracted for, and a designation with no reach problem still needs the retrieval architecture. They are complements, and the manuscript can say so in one sentence.

The cost that remains is real: uniformity of the field dictionary is a bet on model-act adoption. Price it in the text rather than let a reader discover it.

Standardization
A single authority could simply specify the evidentiary protocol.
Objection
On the evidence side, one authority could designate the model, the prompt set, and the thresholds — eliminating the methodological contest that Majoritarian Signals concedes it cannot standardize, and which it abandons to case-by-case adjudication for reasons of technical churn, obsolescence, and compliance circularity MS, p. 68.

Answer
Designation by a single authority is the least robust answer available, and their own evidence shows why.

If one model is designated, its training corpus and post-training alignment become an unreviewable source of legal meaning. That paper documents precisely the exposure: alignment interventions materially shift majoritarian outputs MS, pp. 21–22; identical queries can yield confidence intervals spanning the full probability range MS, p. 23; and models risk conflating what is with what ought to be MS, n. 71. Designation does not remove the normative choice. It relocates it into a procurement decision and removes the mechanism by which the substitution could be caught.

Contestability is therefore not a stylistic preference for adversarial process; it is the only falsification mechanism available for an is/ought substitution. That argument rescues their case-by-case default on a better ground than technological flux: case-by-case is expensive but falsifiable, designated protocol cheap and unfalsifiable.

The residual problem is distributive, not jurisdictional. That paper asserts twice MS, pp. 29, 49 that adversarial testing will discipline methodology, without reaching whether both parties can afford to test. This is where the front door's own logic transfers: an ex ante protocol specification — model, version, run count, prompt set, reported interval — fixed before dispute does for methodological access what an ex ante record duty does for factual access. Fixed protocol is to contested methodology as fixed record is to reconstructed record.

03 · Adversarial compliance
The rule specifies a description, not a measure.
Goodhart's law operates on proxies: name a performance target and the target is optimized while the goal is abandoned. The front door names no performance. It names which facts about choices already made must be written down — which version ran, who could stop it, who holds the record. Those are not scores. They are descriptions of organizational structure, and the only way to move a description is to move the thing described.

That is the reply to the deepest version of the capture objection, and it is worth making in the text rather than leaving to a reader who has already decided the Act is a compliance regime.

The first-order evasion the Act closes is the black box: after designation, no operator can answer that the system is unexplainable or that the data does not exist. What follows is second-order — evasion of the record rule itself. Each of the three available moves either fails on the Act's own terms or succeeds at the price of substantive-law exposure.
Evasion one · Slicing practical authority
Fragment control until no entity satisfies the material-control test.
The move
Distribute update, suspension, monitoring and audit rights across the stack in slices too thin for § 5(b), and disclaim the remainder by contract. If no lane exists, Stage Two reaches nobody.

On the Act's terms
Stage One does not run on control. The visible operator answers because it is visible — its gateway role "follows from visibility and provenance, not a presumption that it controlled every risk" p. 19. Slicing cannot reach the first answer; it can only attack Stage Two, and there it is self-defeating. § 4's custodian map must list every entity with update, restriction, rollback, suspension or termination authority, so finer slicing yields a longer map. Nor do contract labels decide the question: Table 1 treats mandatory updates, cross-tenant disabling and monitoring as evidence of a lane retained "despite contract language assigning all responsibility downstream" p. 22.

Under governing law
Because the test is functional, the slice has to be real. An enterprise that genuinely cannot update, constrain or suspend a deployed high-impact system has surrendered a capability it wants on commercial and safety grounds. The cost of the evasion is the capability itself. And the exposure does not vanish — it lands on the visible operator, which cannot slice, and which § 2 already obliges to secure retrieval rights before deployment.

Evasion two · Compliance flooding
Answer completely, unusably, and at volume.
The move
Satisfy § 4 by producing everything — fifty thousand pages, two hundred custodians, every conceivable supplier named. Technically responsive, practically unusable, and expensive enough to end the matter.

On the Act's terms
Stage One is a form, not a production. § 4 asks for an event receipt and a custodian map, and Part IV.B keeps both as a "closed manifest" p. 46 in the designation's own field definitions. A version identifier buried in volume has not been identified. The Act already names three members of this family — silence, blanket proprietary designation, referral to an unnamed vendor p. 20 — under a standard that reaches an operator who "refuses or materially underanswers." § 6 forecloses the bulk objection: objections run field by field on facts within the objector's control, and every uncontested field remains due.

Under governing law
Over-naming buys nothing either. § 5's negative rules exclude investment, general cloud service, research, brand association and theoretical capacity, so surplus entities exit under safe exit at the operator's expense and its suppliers' cost. The structural answer sits earlier still: the designation fixes the Stage One universe before the event, so two claimants with different liability theories receive identical fields p. 34. Volume cannot be tuned to the plaintiff.

Evasion three · Strategic incompetence
Architect away the ability to intervene, then claim safe exit.
The move
Build so that no one can update, pause or stop the deployment. § 5(b) practical authority fails, Table 1's termination column recognizes "technical ability prohibited and unused," and the enterprise exits the front-door role.

On the Act's terms
This one works, and it is meant to. Safe exit is available and Table 1 recognizes exactly this showing. But the exit is not free of record. § 2 and Table 3's intervention row require the deployment record to identify who may restrict, revoke credentials, update, pause, roll back or terminate — with triggers, response times and tested procedures. If the answer is that no one may, that fact is recorded, dated and attributable before any injury occurs, and Table 3's evaluation row separately requires rejected mitigations and unresolved risk decisions to be logged as what they are.

Under governing law
§ 8 bars inference from the Act's own categories — coverage, notice, compliance, noncompliance, participation. It expressly preserves everything else: "Independently relevant operational conduct retains only the effect governing law gives it" p. 22. Deploying a high-impact system into an employment, clinical or benefits process that no one can stop is operational conduct, not a compliance fact. The enterprise buys exit from the information role with a dated admission that, under negligence, products or agency theory, is not a defense but the case.

The cheapest route to front-door compliance is retaining real control, and retaining real control is also the best merits defense Part IV.F. That is what makes the design incentive-compatible rather than merely burdensome — and it is currently an emergent property of the drafting rather than a stated claim.
§ 4 fixes content but not form
Part IV.B builds a standard interface and a sectoral data dictionary, but Model Act § 4 requires only that the fields be supplied, not that they be supplied in that interface. This is the one live variant of flooding: every field delivered, in a shape expensive to parse. One clause closes it — the answer is due in the form the designation specifies.

The material-change threshold is assigned twice
§ 2 gives the material-change rule to the designation. Part IV.A reads as though the operator sets it: the control sheet "should identify which changes require renewed evaluation or approval and which enter a lower-burden change log" p. 46. Left standing, that is a real metric and therefore really gameable — route every change into the low-burden log. Conform Part IV.A to § 2: the sheet applies the designation's threshold, it does not set it.

The incentive structure needs a merits claim behind it
Everything above depends on governing law supplying something for the record to feed. p. 53 concedes that some losses have no private remedy. Where none exists, strategic incompetence goes unpunished: the Act supplies the exit and nothing catches what walks through it. This is the design's real boundary, and it reads better stated than found.

04 · What to concede
Two trades that read better stated than discovered.
Trade one
Designation dependency
The machinery is inert until a legislature designates. n. 19 already concedes that California's companion-chatbot provisions demonstrate the form without containing the front-door rule. A reader will press the gap: you have shown that legislatures can designate, not that they will designate the fields that bind.

Answer by scope. The Article's claim is that the fields are specifiable, administrable, and bounded — not that adoption is assured. Say so, and the objection converts into a limit the Article set for itself.

Recital 2 of Reg. (EU) 2026/1744 sharpens why this concession has to be explicit. Stakeholder consultation there reopened an already enacted timetable. Designation dependency is therefore not only a question of whether a legislature acts, but of whether what it enacts survives the implementation period intact — which is an argument for fixing the field list in the designation itself rather than delegating it onward.

Trade two
Thin deterrence
§ 9's rule that bare nonperformance creates no private statutory damages, together with § 8's merits firewall, is the Article's cleanest analytical move and its largest enforcement liability. A duty that cannot be converted into damages is a duty that is cheap to ignore.

The trade buys adoptability — industry cannot say the Act creates liability — at the price of deterrent force. It is defensible on its own terms, and stating it forecloses the reading that the Article did not notice.

06 · Before cite-check
Four items to verify against primary sources.
AI Omnibus deferrals and recital 2 verified
Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, published OJ 24 July 2026, in force 27 July 2026. Annex III standalone high-risk obligations deferred to 2 December 2027; Annex I to 2 August 2028. Recital 2 has been read verbatim on EUR-Lex and is quoted exactly in section 02. Two drafting cautions: cite it as a recital, which states reasons and aids interpretation rather than binding; and do not compress it into "the burden was too heavy," because the recital attributes the heavier burden to delayed standards and delayed national conformity-assessment frameworks, not to the intrinsic cost of the duties. That distinction is the whole value of the citation.

Article 86 timing — check before asserting
Article 86 was not among the deferred provisions and applies from 2 August 2026, while the Chapter III documentation and logging apparatus it would draw on is deferred for Annex III systems. If that reading survives the Regulation's transitional terms, it is the single best comparative illustration of the Article's thesis: an access right that arrives before the record duty does no work. Do not assert it until the transitional provisions are read directly.

AI Liability Directive withdrawal — date unverified
Withdrawal was signalled in the Commission Work Programme 2025 and subsequently effected, but this note did not confirm the operative date. Establish it from the Work Programme and the withdrawal notice before citing, and cite the original 2022 proposal's disclosure article for the mechanism itself.

Civil-law pre-action devices
Confirm current article numbering and scope for CPC art. 145 and ZPO §§ 485–494a, and keep the characterization narrow — they are evidence-securing procedures, cited as institutional precedent for a bounded pre-action stage, not as equivalents of the front door.
