\begin{abstract}
Courts already decide disputes involving artificial intelligence, yet a threshold premise remains unsettled: what, legally, is the system before the court? Legislatures regulate uses and providers without supplying a stable jurisprudential account. Firms fill the gap with metaphors---assistant, companion, agent, tool---that can migrate unnoticed into legal analysis.

This Article supplies a minimum structure that does not depend on resolving machine consciousness. It separates two questions. One is invariant across ordinary doctrine: absent positive law creating a new juridical status, an AI system is not an independent bearer of legal claims, powers, duties, and liabilities. The other is functional: the same system may be a product, service, instrument, communicative medium, or professional component because each classification answers a different doctrinal question. Stable legal subjects and variable legal objects make apparently inconsistent AI cases coherent.

The Article then reconstructs responsibility around deployment. A deployment-control cascade identifies who sets objectives, supplies resources, configures models and tools, receives risk information, and can stop operation. That map translates into duty, notice, breach, causation, and proof. A two-layer architecture makes the deploying enterprise the claimant-facing responsibility center while an ex ante charter allocates authority, protects good-faith escalation, preserves evidence, and permits internal recourse. The result makes probabilistic control visible.
\end{abstract}
